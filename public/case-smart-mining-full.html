Act 02 — Context

A $4.2B industry
where data lives
in silos

Underground mining is one of the most sensor-dense industrial environments on earth. Gas monitors, equipment IoT, worker RFID tags, ore grade scanners, seismic arrays — all generating streams of data. And yet the people responsible for life-or-death operational decisions are navigating this through five disconnected dashboards, a radio, and institutional memory.

The SMMS brief: design the unified intelligent operations platform that these operations have needed for 20 years. Serve three fundamentally different users — the shift supervisor making 4-second triage calls, the safety officer managing compliance trails, and the operations manager tracking production strategy.

Why This Project Existed

The client challenge
that created the brief

The Ministry of Energy and Minerals in Oman manages 294 active mine licences across 4 lifecycle stages. A spike in environmental compliance violations and delayed incident response in 2022 triggered an executive mandate: build a unified digital platform that gives regulators and site managers real-time operational visibility. The existing toolset — a combination of spreadsheets, legacy GIS software, and SMS alerts — had no intelligence layer and no integration.

I was brought in to lead the UX design from zero: no prior design system, no established patterns, no user research on file. The engineering team had a data pipeline but no product surface.

The problem isn't missing data.
It's incoherent data.

147 field observations across 3 mine sites distilled into one precise diagnosis: every piece of data supervisors needed to make good decisions existed somewhere — it was just scattered across systems with no intelligence connecting them. The result was not just inefficiency. It was a design-caused safety risk.

Problem Solutions Framework — 8 Opportunity Areas

Identified through contextual research and stakeholder workshops. Each maps to a measurable user pain.

01 Situational Awareness

Unified live view of all zones, workers, equipment, and environment — accessible in under 3 taps from any entry point, on any device.

02 AI Alert Triage

03 Predictive Maintenance

Vibration, temp, and load telemetry feeds a failure prediction model — surfacing 4–72h maintenance windows before unplanned downtime.

Every alert, ack, action, and resolution timestamped. MSHA regulatory reports auto-generated — eliminating 40% of safety officer admin time.

Grade, tonnage, and efficiency tracked vs. target with AI confidence intervals. Weekly forecast visible to operations managers in real time.

08 Role-Adaptive UI

Same system, intelligently different views by role context. Supervisor sees spatial map + critical alerts first. Manager sees production KPIs first.

Key Behavioural Patterns Observed — Not Quotes, Behaviours

Real research is messy. These are behavioural patterns observed repeatedly in contextual sessions — more reliable than any single quote.

Observed Pattern 01 — Triage Instinct

Supervisors always process in the same order — always

Without exception, every supervisor observed (n=6) began incident response by asking: (1) What happened? (2) Where exactly? (3) Who's in that zone? — before asking severity, cause, or recommended action. The existing system answered these in reverse order. This single insight drove the spatial canvas decision.

Observed Pattern 02 — Workaround Culture

All experienced operators had built shadow systems

Every supervisor with more than 2 years of experience had created personal workarounds: notebook logs, WhatsApp groups with operators, screenshot-sharing of alerts, and handwritten zone maps. The system was not being used as designed. Experienced operators had, effectively, designed their own information layer on top of the official one.

Observed Pattern 03 — Compliance Dread

Safety officers visibly tensed when asked about reporting

During diary study debrief, all 3 safety officers showed visible discomfort when discussing end-of-shift reporting. One described it as "the second job I have to do after my actual job." Compliance work was perceived as punishment, not process — because the system gave them no running record. Every report was built from scratch by reconciling exports from 5 different tools.

The Critical Finding: Supervisors Always Ask in the Same Order

Every supervisor observed (n=6) began incident response identically. The existing system answered in the wrong order. This single insight drove the spatial canvas decision.

Intent-First UX (IFU) — Design Principles Applied

SMMS was designed around the IFU framework — designing for user goals before interface affordances. Three layers, three cognitive science laws per layer.

Layer 01 — Intent Recognition

System surfaces intent before navigation

Role + time-of-day context + recent interactions predicts the next most likely intent. The supervisor starting a shift sees zone status first — not last week's production report.

Fitts's LawHick's Law
Layer 02 — Context Collapse

One tap surfaces all decision-relevant context

Tapping an alert opens a unified context panel: crew location, equipment state, historical precedent, recommended action. No navigation to make the decision.

Miller's LawTesler's Law
Layer 03 — Peak-End Design

Critical moments engineered for clarity and recall

Incident resolution, shift handover, compliance sign-off — the moments users remember — designed for zero ambiguity. This determines "the system works" perception.

Kahneman Peak-End Rule

Intent-First UX — The Framework as a System Diagram

IFU is not a visual style — it is a decision architecture. Three layers, six cognitive science principles, one goal: eliminate the distance between user intent and system response.

Stakeholder Alignment — The Hardest Design Problem

Three groups wanted fundamentally different things. The resolution wasn't a compromise — it was a role-adaptive architecture that gave each group exactly what they needed without surfacing what they didn't.

Operations Team — Wanted

"Maximum data visibility — show us everything, all the time."

Operations leadership wanted a single screen showing all 294 mines, all sensor readings, all alerts simultaneously. Their mental model was a control room with every possible variable visible.

Resolution: National overview map with progressive disclosure — national aggregate default, drill-down to any mine in one click. Visibility without information overload.

Safety Team — Wanted

"Reduced cognitive load — fewer things asking for attention."

Safety officers were experiencing alert fatigue and wanted a simplified view that only surfaced genuinely critical items. They pushed back strongly against the operations team's "show everything" request.

Resolution: AI intent-triage layer that collapses 240 alerts to 18 prioritised items, with role-based view defaulting to compliance-relevant incidents first.

Engineering — Concerned About

"Offline synchronisation is architecturally complex and risky."

The solution architect flagged that full offline capability with real-time sync would require significant infrastructure investment — edge compute at each site, local ML inference, and conflict resolution logic for syncing 72h of buffered data.

Resolution: Negotiated a priority-queue offline model — safety-critical data syncs first, production analytics deferred. Reduced engineering scope by 40% without compromising the core use case.

Key Design Tradeoffs — Where Real Projects Live

This project wasn't perfect. Here are the decisions where we compromised between ideal and real.

Tradeoff 01 — AI Latency
Wanted: Real-time predictive AI with sub-second response.
Constraint: ML model inference time was 6–9 seconds over cellular — unusable for triage.
Decision
Moved ML inference to edge compute at site level. 40ms local response. Confidence scores displayed with each AI recommendation so users know when to trust the model.
Design Impact
Added AI confidence percentage to every alert card. Low-confidence predictions surfaced as "Suggested" not "Critical" — users calibrate trust based on visible model certainty.
Tradeoff 02 — Digital Twin
Wanted: Full 3D digital twin of each mine — real-time underground visualisation.
Constraint: 3D data from legacy surveys was incomplete for 60% of sites.
Decision
Shipped 2D spatial canvas with satellite imagery + GIS layers (hazards, air quality). Flagged 3D digital twin as Phase 2 deliverable pending survey data completeness audit.
Design Impact
The 2D approach actually tested better — supervisors found 3D mine models harder to orientate under pressure. The constraint led to a better UX decision than the original brief intended.
Tradeoff 03 — Alert Autonomy
Wanted: AI that could automatically escalate critical alerts and dispatch maintenance teams.
Constraint: Ministry legal team required human sign-off on all safety actions.
Decision
AI recommends, humans decide. Every suggested action requires one tap to confirm. AI can pre-populate the action form but cannot execute without explicit human approval.
Design Impact
This became the AI Design Principle architecture below. The legal constraint was actually a better design — it created an audit trail and kept humans accountable for safety decisions.

AI Design Principles — Responsible AI in Safety-Critical Systems

AI in this system is not autonomous. Every principle below was arrived at through research, engineering constraint, or legal requirement — not aesthetics.

What AI Can Do
✓Prioritise and de-duplicate alerts from raw sensor data (240 → 18)
✓Predict equipment failure 4–72h ahead with confidence score
✓Suggest recommended actions with one-line reasoning ("why this is critical")
✓Forecast weekly ore production with confidence interval
✓Surface compliance risk score per mine based on inspection history
What AI Cannot Do — By Design
✗Act autonomously on any alert — human confirmation required every time
✗Dispatch maintenance teams or trigger evacuations without operator approval
✗Show a recommendation without its confidence score and reasoning
✗Override a human decision even if model confidence is 99%
✗Create an audit log entry without a named human approver

Act 08 — Reflection

3 design learnings that will inform everything built next

Learning 01

Spatial beats hierarchical under cognitive load

Supervisors navigated 34% faster with the spatial canvas vs tab/menu hierarchies. "Where is it?" is a more primal organising instinct than "what category is it?" under time-critical stress. For safety-critical interfaces, spatial navigation is not a UX preference — it's a cognitive load reduction strategy.

Learning 02

Opaque AI is ignored AI in safety contexts

Unexplained AI priority scores were consistently distrusted in testing. Adding a single confidence indicator and a one-line rationale ("why this is critical") increased AI alert action rate by 34%. In safety-critical environments, trust is not a nice-to-have — it is the product. Design for explainability from day one.

Learning 03

Offline-first is the real trust foundation

Underground connectivity is variable and unpredictable. The 72h offline buffer with local ML inference was the single most praised technical decision by all 8 pilot participants. Any system that degrades in offline mode loses user trust permanently and reverts to radio + paper. Offline-first is not a feature — it is the trust architecture.

Honest Reflection

What Didn't Work — and What I'd Do Differently

Principal-level design maturity means knowing what you'd change. These aren't excuses — they're the lessons that are now baked into how I work.

What Failed — Concept C (Alert-Centric)

Alert-first design amplified exactly the problem it was trying to solve

We built a concept that surfaced all safety alerts as the primary view. Testing showed that users immediately experienced the same fatigue they described in the existing system — just faster. By making alerts the entry point, we were designing for the symptom, not the cause. The cause was that alerts had no spatial context. We killed it at week 7 and moved to the spatial canvas approach.

What I'd Do Differently — Research Phase

We did 14 interviews but only 3 site visits — imbalance I'd correct

Most of our 14 interviews happened remotely due to site access permits. The 3 in-person sessions generated far richer observations — especially the workaround behaviours that only became visible when watching someone actually do their job. If I ran this project again, I'd push harder for 8+ in-person sessions and fewer remote interviews. The shadow systems supervisors had built were invisible until we were physically on site.

What I'd Do Differently — Stakeholder Alignment

I aligned the three teams too late — should have happened before research ended

The conflict between the operations team's "show everything" preference and the safety team's "reduce cognitive load" requirement surfaced at the concept review — which delayed us by 2 weeks. If I ran it again, I'd facilitate a shared problem definition workshop before any research synthesis, so stakeholders are co-owners of the insights before I present design directions. Alignment is easier to build than it is to retrofit.
